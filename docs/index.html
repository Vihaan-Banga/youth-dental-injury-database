Youth Sports Dental Injury Database

Dental and mouth injuries in young athletes are reported across dozens of separate studies and surveillance systems that measure them in incompatible ways. This database brings 421 published findings into one place and, just as importantly, records which of them can validly be compared with each other and which cannot.

The problem

Two studies can both report a “dental injury rate” and mean entirely different things

Researchers measure injuries in whatever way suits their study: per athlete-exposure, per season, per 100,000 emergency-department visits, or as a plain percentage of a squad. Those numbers look comparable sitting next to each other. They usually are not. Some published “injury rates” in dental research are not even restricted to dental injuries.

Published figureWhat it actually measures
22.5 per 1,000 collegiate rugby — all injuries, dental merely included
0.026 per 1,000 high-school basketball — dental injuries only

Both are real, correctly reported figures measured per athlete-exposure. Placing them side by side would be wrong by roughly a thousandfold. The database flags the first one so that cannot happen.

What the database does

Collect, classify, and refuse invalid comparisons

01 — Collect

One format, 103 sources

Peer-reviewed studies, 12 years of US emergency-department surveillance, and a governing-body report, extracted into a single schema of 40 fields with every original citation preserved.

02 — Classify

Nine kinds of evidence

Every finding is labelled with the kind of measure it reports — an exposure-based rate, a prevalence proportion, an ED estimate, a raw count — and grouped with the findings it may legitimately be compared against.

03 — Refuse

Incompatible stays incompatible

Of 129 findings reporting a rate, only 20 share a common denominator. The other 109 are deliberately left un-normalized rather than forced onto one scale.

Why that matters

The comparability rules are part of the data, not a footnote

Most evidence summaries discuss these differences in prose, where nothing stops a reader from pooling numbers that should never be pooled. Here the judgement is attached to every row, written in a form software can read, and checked automatically each time the dataset is rebuilt.

9 measure types

Each finding is tagged with the basis on which it was measured.

109 of 129 rates left un-normalized

Only genuinely athlete-exposure-based rates are placed on a shared scale.

19 findings flagged

Published figures whose “injury rate” is broader than dental injury, so they are never read as one.

14 automated checks

Rules run on every update; a violation stops the build rather than reaching a user.

None of this is new epidemiology. Distinguishing incidence from prevalence is standard practice. What is unusual is making those distinctions machine-readable and enforced, so an invalid comparison is blocked rather than merely discouraged.

What you can find in it

Three examples, each traceable to its source

Roughly three in four high-school athletes who sustained a dental injury were not wearing a mouthguard.

459 dental injuries across 49,987,927 athlete-exposures, US high-school surveillance, 2005/06–2019/20. Azadani et al., Dental Traumatology (2023).

Basketball carries the highest dental injury rate among boys’ high-school sports; girls’ field hockey the highest among girls’.

2.4 and 3.5 dental injuries per 100,000 athlete-exposures respectively, same surveillance programme. Read each rate together with its denominator.

In one study of college basketball, players without custom-fitted mouthguards recorded 0.67 dental injuries per 1,000 athlete-exposures, against 0.12 for those wearing them.

Single season, 1999–2000, small injury counts. LaBella et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (2002). One study, not a pooled estimate.

Mouthguard use is recorded in only 41 of the 421 findings, which is itself a finding: the question most relevant to prevention is the one the literature answers least often.

Read this before using the data

  • These are published findings, not patient records. Each entry is a statistic reported by a study or surveillance system, for example “459 dental injuries across nearly 50 million athlete-exposures.” Entries must not be summed or counted as individual injuries.
  • The evidence is mostly American. 28 countries appear, but US sources supply about 72% of the findings. Results may not transfer elsewhere.
  • Most sources never say which injury occurred. 84% of findings report only “dental injury” without distinguishing avulsion, fracture, or luxation.
  • The numbers are not yet independently verified. Every entry is flagged as not fully verified. Values have been checked for internal arithmetic consistency and, where the text allows, against each study’s abstract, but checking against full-text articles is still in progress. Extraction was carried out by a single reviewer with AI assistance, and no second reviewer has yet re-screened a sample.
  • Not a clinical decision tool. This describes populations and cannot predict any individual patient’s risk.

How it was built

Written down, logged, and reproducible

Sources were found through systematic PubMed and journal searches, US Consumer Product Safety Commission data, and governing-body reports, then screened against a written protocol: ages 5–22, sport-related, dental or orofacial injury, published in 2000 or later. Every judgement call that was not obvious is recorded, dated, and explained — 27 entries so far. The whole dataset rebuilds from its source files with a single command, and the automated checks run on every change.

Using and citing it

Free to use, with attribution

This is a pre-release version. You are welcome to explore the data and use it to find the underlying research. For clinical statements or publications, please cite the original studies — every entry carries its full citation — rather than database-wide totals.
